# Output Contract

The final decision packet should be useful enough that a buyer can act without another generic comparison.

## 1. Verdict

Begin with one direct sentence:

`Stay with [current setup]`, `Pilot [candidate] beside your current setup`, `Move to [candidate]`, or `Build a narrow owned layer around [engines]`.

Name the operating path before the vendor.

## 2. Current-state diagnosis

State:

- what the user is trying to accomplish
- what is actually failing
- whether the urge to move is pain-led, requirement-led, or hype-led
- the cost of doing nothing
- the cost of moving

## 3. Requirement hierarchy

Split the requirements into:

- non-negotiable
- high-value
- nice-to-have
- social-media attraction with no proven workflow value

## 4. Evidence-backed fit table

Show the user-specific weights and scores. Include separate default and ceiling assessments. Attach `F`, `O`, `A`, or `U` to material scores.

## 5. Recommendation

Explain the three strongest reasons the selected path fits. Tie every reason to an answer or current source.

## 6. Mode-aware alternative

- For a full audit, name the runner-up and the exact constraint that made it lose. State what answer or product change would reverse the order.
- For a switch audit, compare `stay` against the named candidate. Do not invent a third option unless it was researched.
- For a product deep dive, state the strongest alternative operating model only if the user's decision requires one.
- For an owned-system blueprint, compare the bounded build with staying on the current stack.

## 7. Why not the rest

Give one useful sentence per serious candidate that was actually researched. Do not make comparative claims about products outside the mode's research scope. Avoid insulting a product that is simply optimized for another operator.

## 8. Defaults versus ceiling

For the recommendation and the mode-appropriate alternative, state:

- what works immediately
- what requires configuration or engineering
- what the vendor operates
- what the user operates
- the realistic ceiling
- the main hidden burden

## 9. Switching ledger

## 10. Reversible 30 to 60 minute test

Design one test around the user's real job. It must:

- reproduce the exact failure or hard requirement that triggered the decision
- use copied or non-sensitive inputs
- avoid deleting or migrating the current system
- include the same acceptance criteria for both systems
- record setup time, intervention count, completion quality, execution time, and failures
- stop before granting broad write access or production credentials

If laptop-closed execution is the deciding requirement, the test must actually close or disconnect the laptop for the relevant interval. If a connector, teammate, scheduled trigger, local file, or approval boundary is decisive, exercise that exact dependency instead of substituting a generic demo.

## 11. Seven-day pilot

Specify:

- three real workflows
- minimum success threshold
- maximum acceptable intervention and maintenance time
- cost or usage tracking
- permission boundary
- fallback path
- clear `adopt`, `extend test`, or `reject` criteria

## 12. Do not switch unless

Name the exact threshold that justifies migration. Example:

`Do not migrate unless the pilot completes the weekly research workflow with at least 30 percent less operator time and no new permission or reliability failure.`

## 13. Sources and confidence

Finish with:

- verification date
- direct first-party links
- open-source release tags and canonical repositories
- unresolved unknowns
- confidence level and what would raise it

Keep verified fact, user observation, and analysis visibly separate.
